\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\input{fig_example}

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is becoming a data management
workload. A production service may access relational tables, sparse and
dense text indexes, vector stores, and remote structured APIs, then apply
fusion, reranking, verification, and generation. The same information need
therefore admits many physical plans. Unlike relational plans, however,
these plans are rarely equivalent: reducing retrieval depth, omitting a
reranker, or using a smaller generator can change the answer. Optimizing
only latency or price is not enough; an optimizer must also determine how
much answer risk the application will accept.

\begin{example}
\label{ex:finance}
A financial assistant joins current figures from an API, tables in company
filings, and analyst commentary from a text index. Its operator requires
that no more than 10\% of answers are incorrect, 10\% contain an
unsupported or stale claim, and 5\% exceed 10 seconds. Many plans appear to
meet these limits on a development set. The operational question is which
plan is cheapest while the service-level limits still hold.
\end{example}

Recent systems expose semantic operators and optimize their physical
implementations~\cite{liu2025palimpzest,patel2024lotus,
shankar2024docetl,russo2025abacus,chai2025quest}. They establish a useful
database abstraction for LLM-based processing, but their feasibility
decisions are based on estimated quality, cost, or latency. An adaptive
router has the same problem: after tuning on a finite sample, the cheapest
apparently feasible configuration is often the one favored by estimation
noise. Conformal RAG methods control the risk of a fixed
pipeline~\cite{li2024traq,kang2024crag}, but do not search a physical-plan
space or optimize cost. The missing component is a plan-selection procedure
whose guarantee remains valid \emph{after} the optimizer has compared many
candidates.

We introduce \emph{evidence contracts}, a declarative interface between an
application and a retrieval-augmented execution engine. A contract specifies
bounded losses---for example, answer error, citation omission, stale
evidence, or latency-budget exceedance---and an admissible rate for each.
\system minimizes metered execution cost subject to these constraints.
The design connects four familiar database concerns:

\textbf{Plan space.} A typed operator algebra represents access to
heterogeneous evidence sources. Cost-reducing, non-equivalent rewrites form
a plan lattice. \emph{Risk vouchers} estimate how often each rewrite harms
an answer and guide enumeration, much as selectivity and cost estimates
guide a classical optimizer.

\textbf{Adaptive physical policies.} Static plans are lifted into
per-query policies: progressive ladders that escalate when current evidence
is insufficient, direct routers, and group-conditional policies. A
cardinality-driven rule chooses between table-first and text-first access
for table--text joins.

\textbf{Certification.} Candidates are ordered using a training split and
tested once on an independent calibration split. A fixed-sequence
Hoeffding--Bentkus procedure certifies the cheapest policy in the passing
prefix. Its failure probability is bounded by $\delta$ regardless of the
number or quality of candidates; estimation errors affect cost, not
contract validity.

\textbf{Runtime control.} An anytime-valid restart-mixture monitor checks
audited violations after deployment. When workload mix, indexes, or
external sources drift, the monitor triggers safe fallback and
recertification on post-change data.

We evaluate \system on HybridQA (tables and text), CRAG (web and structured
APIs), and ALCE-ASQA/QAMPARI (long-form and list answers with citation
constraints). All plans use real token- or GPU-time-metered executions.
Across 1000 repeated calibration draws, we evaluate the selected policy on
the exact risk of the pooled finite population rather than on one noisy
test split. \system stays within the $\delta=0.1$ deployment-failure budget
at all thirteen tested contract settings, with a worst observed rate of
0.8\%. Point-estimate tuning, Bayesian optimization, routing, and Abacus
reach 64\%. At representative contracts, \system costs
$6.9\times$ less than the strongest fixed plan on HybridQA,
$20\times$ less on CRAG, and $15\times$ less on QAMPARI. It also detects
an injected CRAG workload shift after a median of 78 queries and certifies
nearly $10^5$ policies in at most 8.5 seconds.

The paper makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a declarative contract and physical-plan model for
  retrieval-augmented data services (\S\ref{sec:contracts});
  \item a query optimizer that combines non-equivalent rewrites,
  selectivity-based risk estimates, adaptive policies, and statistically
  valid certification (\S\ref{sec:optimizer}); and
  \item progressive execution and drift-aware recertification, evaluated
  end to end on four heterogeneous benchmarks
  (\S\ref{sec:runtime}--\S\ref{sec:experiments}).
\end{itemize}
