\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Research questions}
\label{sec:rqs}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1 (does the contract hold?)} Over served streams, does
the realised violation rate stay below $\alpha$ at \emph{every} prefix,
for the ledger and for a one-shot certifier, and what happens to each
when the stream is not exchangeable with the calibration prefix?
\item \textbf{RQ2 (what does it cost?)} How much does each method spend
per query -- serving, declining and auditing -- relative to an offline
optimum that already knows every plan's risk?
\item \textbf{RQ3 (which ingredient matters?)} Declining, randomisation
and the ledger are three separable changes. What does each contribute,
and in particular, how much of the gain is explained by declining alone?
\item \textbf{RQ4 (is it efficient, and how fast?)} Does the cost gap to
the offline optimum decay at the $O(T^{-1/2})$ rate of
Theorem~\ref{thm:regret}, and how sensitive is the controller to its one
constant $\kappa$?
\item \textbf{RQ5 (what do labels cost?)} Auditing every served query is
what makes the guarantee deterministic. What is the price of auditing
less, under each of the two accounting rules?
\item \textbf{RQ6 (does the plan space help?)} Unbundling access path
from generator gives a $36$-plan grid on HybridQA and a $24$-plan grid
on CRAG. Does a larger space help a static certifier, does it hurt it,
and can an operator tell which in advance?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Datasets and contracts}
\label{sec:datasets}

Four heterogeneous benchmarks, all executions real and metered.
(A) \textbf{HybridQA} \citep{chen2020hybridqa}: multi-hop QA over
Wikipedia tables joined with entity passages; sources are a structured
row-selection operator and sparse/dense passage indexes over 440K
passages; loss $\mathbf{1}[\text{token-F1} < 0.5]$; history/stream
$= 5000/6466$.
(B) \textbf{CRAG} \citep{yang2024cragbench}: web QA with the official
mock knowledge-graph API over five domains; answers graded by an LLM
judge against the gold answer following CRAG's protocol; loss
$\mathbf{1}[\text{not correct}]$; history/stream $= 700/2006$.
(C) \textbf{ASQA} \citep{stelmakh2022asqa} under the official ALCE
evaluation \citep{gao2023alce}: long-form answers with citations over a
pooled GTR corpus, citation recall scored by the official TRUE-NLI
entailment model \citep{honovich2022true}; loss
$\mathbf{1}[\text{citation recall} < 50]$; history/stream $= 150/798$.
(D) \textbf{QAMPARI} \citep{amouyal2022qampari} under the same protocol:
list answers over an 86K-passage corpus; same loss; history/stream
$= 150/850$.

The four span the range that matters for this paper. The risk floor
$\min_p r_p$ is $0.12$ on ASQA, $0.27$ on HybridQA and $0.59$ on both
CRAG and QAMPARI, so contract levels are chosen per task to straddle
that floor: levels below it are unattainable without declining and are
marked as such throughout. Stream lengths differ by a factor of eight,
which is what lets us separate the asymptotic behaviour of the
controller from its transient.

\subsection{Plans, the plan grid, and cost metering}
\label{sec:ladders}

The base plan space per benchmark is a four-rung cost-ascending ladder:
from sparse-only retrieval with a flash-tier generator up to hybrid
reciprocal-rank-fusion retrieval, cross-encoder reranking, NLI
verification, knowledge-graph-API evidence (CRAG), citation repair
(ASQA, QAMPARI) and a max-tier generator. Adding the per-query routing
and mixture candidates of Section~\ref{sec:optimise} gives $69$
candidate policies, of which the Pareto frontier on historical data
retains $10$--$15$; both methods are given the same frontier, so the
comparison is not about screening.

\paragraph{The plan grid (RQ6)}
The ladder is a hand-drawn diagonal through a larger space: retrieval
strength and generator tier rise together, so it never contains a plan
that retrieves deeply and generates cheaply. We therefore also executed
the full Cartesian product of access paths and generators on two
benchmarks. On HybridQA the product is four access paths $\times$ nine
generators: six hosted, spanning three model developers and three price
tiers (Qwen flash/plus/max, DeepSeek v4-flash/v3.2, GLM~4.7), and three
open-weight models served locally (Gemma~3 4b, Gemma~3 12b,
Llama~3.1~8B), giving $36$ physical plans, Pareto pruned to $8$ on the
training split. On CRAG the six hosted generators give $24$ plans,
pruned to $5$, with answers labelled by the same judge as the CRAG
ladder so that the grid and the ladder are scored identically. Each
access path's retrieval view is materialised once and shared by every
generator, so retrieval operators are paid once, which is the RAG
analogue of a materialised common subexpression; only generation is
metered per plan. Reporting a second grid matters because the effect of
enlarging the space turns out to have a task-dependent sign
(\S\ref{sec:res:grid}), which a single benchmark would have hidden.

\paragraph{Metering}
The physical layer uses DuckDB \citep{raasveldt2019duckdb} for
structured sources, BM25 \citep{robertson2009bm25} for sparse
retrieval, BGE embeddings \citep{xiao2023bge} with FAISS
\citep{johnson2019faiss} for dense retrieval, reciprocal-rank fusion
\citep{cormack2009rrf}, a bge-reranker-v2-m3 cross-encoder and
DeBERTa-based NLI verification; local generators run on two RTX~4090
GPUs. Hosted calls are token-metered at each developer's list prices;
local calls are metered in GPU-seconds at an RTX-4090 rental rate, so
hosted and local plans share one currency. All costs are milli-CNY
(mCNY) per query. Every LLM and API call passes through a thread-safe
cache keyed by (model, messages, decoding parameters), so every table
regenerates from the artifact with no further calls.

\paragraph{The price of declining}
$c_\bot$ is a property of the deployment, not of the method, so we sweep
it at $\{0.5, 1, 2\}\times$ the cost of the most expensive plan in the
space. All tables report the middle setting unless stated; the sweep is
in Section~\ref{sec:res:cost}. Pricing a decline at the strongest plan's
cost is the conservative reading: it says a human handoff costs about
what the best pipeline costs, so the optimiser gets no free lunch from
declining.

\subsection{Protocol and baselines}
\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{The stream}
The pooled calibration and test executions form a finite population; one
\emph{draw} is an ordering of it. Both systems serve the same stream and
are charged for everything they consume. This is the point where the
usual comparison is unfair to no one but the reader: a one-shot
certifier looks cheap only if the queries it burns on calibration are
free, and they are not -- they are real queries, served without a
certificate, by whatever plan the history liked. We therefore charge the
certifier's calibration prefix at the historically safest plan and
charge its labels, exactly as we charge the ledger's.

\paragraph{Baselines}
\emph{LTT, no decline} is fixed-sequence Learn-then-Test
\citep{angelopoulos2021ltt} with Hoeffding--Bentkus $p$-values over the
$69$ candidates, $n_{\mathrm{cal}} = 500$, $\delta = 0.1$ -- the
incumbent formulation and the method of our own earlier design.
\emph{LTT + decline} is the same procedure with $\bot$ added to the
candidate space, including mixtures of a plan with $\bot$ on a
$9$-point grid; this is the baseline that isolates our contribution from
the mere availability of declining. \emph{Ledger, deterministic plan} is
Algorithm~\ref{alg:ledger} restricted to single plans, which can reach
only frontier vertices. \emph{Offline action LP} solves \eqref{eq:alp}
on the population risks and costs: it knows what no deployed system
knows and buys no labels, so it is a lower bound rather than a
competitor, and it is what "$\times$ oracle" means throughout.

\paragraph{Reported quantities}
For each configuration we run $20$--$30$ independent streams and report
the mean per-query cost (serving $+$ declines $+$ audit), the realised
risk over the whole stream, the decline rate, the \emph{worst prefix
rate} $\max_{t \ge 200} V_t/t$, and the \emph{breach} fraction -- the
share of streams whose worst prefix rate exceeded $\alpha$. The worst
prefix rate is the quantity Definition~\ref{def:enforcing} controls and
that Definition~\ref{def:certified} says nothing about; the first $200$
arrivals are excluded because no method can control a rate over a
handful of queries, and the exclusion is applied identically to both.
